# Cannabis and Criminalisation in South Africa

## Purpose

This collection documents the historical development of cannabis regulation,
policing, criminalisation, prosecution, constitutional litigation and
criminal-record consequences in South Africa.

The purpose is not to advocate for or against cannabis policy.

Path should distinguish:

- documented historical fact
- primary-source evidence
- scholarly interpretation
- government position
- legal judgment
- unresolved historical claims

## Core historical question

How did cannabis move from a widely used plant into a heavily criminalised
substance, and how did that criminalisation interact with policing, race,
urbanisation, labour control and later constitutional rights?

## 1922: National criminalisation

Cannabis was formally policed as a dangerous drug in South Africa from 1922.

Historical research identifies the 1922 Customs and Excise Amendment as an
important point in the national criminalisation of cannabis.

The 1928 Medical, Dental and Pharmacy Act subsequently prohibited a range of
activities involving habit-forming drugs and plants, including possession,
cultivation, supply and use.

Source:
Nkosi, Devey and Waetjen, "Cannabis policing in mid-twentieth century
South Africa", Historia, 2020.

## Cannabis and policing

Historical police records show substantial numbers of cannabis arrests during
the twentieth century.

The available research identifies 5,762 cannabis arrests in 1932 and reports
that annual arrest numbers approximately doubled during the 1940s.

The historical record also shows that urban policing frequently involved
possession and small-scale dealing, while later policing increasingly focused
on cultivation, supply and transport.

Path must not convert these historical statistics into claims about all
cannabis arrests or all policing without identifying the relevant period,
source and counting method.

## Race, urbanisation and social control

Historical scholarship places cannabis policing within the wider system of
colonial and segregation-era policing.

The research describes cannabis control as ancillary to a broader collection
of laws and policing practices regulating urban spaces, African mobility and
racial boundaries.

This should be presented as a documented scholarly interpretation supported
by historical evidence, rather than as a claim that every cannabis arrest
was politically motivated.

## The 1952 Committee on the Abuse of Dagga

The 1952 governmental committee examined cannabis as a social problem and
recommended changes to policing strategy.

Historical research reports that the committee described existing dagga
policing as often arising from police activity directed at other offences,
with searches for evidence of other crimes sometimes resulting in cannabis
being discovered.

This is particularly relevant to research into the relationship between
general policing and cannabis possession offences.

Path should quote or closely paraphrase the underlying historical source
rather than generalising this finding into a claim that police routinely
used cannabis charges against political opponents.

## 1960s

Historical police statistics continued to record large numbers of cannabis
prosecutions.

For the 1963/64 reporting year, historical research reports 25,911
prosecutions for dagga offences, with approximately 90 percent involving
possession.

This figure is specific to the cited reporting period and offence
classification. It must not be presented as a contemporary statistic.

## 1971

The Abuse of Dependency-Producing Substances and Rehabilitation Centres Act
introduced a substantially more punitive drug-control framework.

Path should distinguish the legislation's text from later historical
interpretations of its social and political effects.

## Constitutional transition

South Africa's constitutional order changed the legal framework within
which cannabis prohibition was assessed.

In 2018, the Constitutional Court confirmed the constitutional invalidity
of legislation criminalising an adult's private use or possession of
cannabis and private cultivation for personal consumption.

Case:

Minister of Justice and Constitutional Development and Others v Prince and
Others, [2018] ZACC 30.

## 2018 Prince judgment

The Constitutional Court's judgment concerned the constitutional right to
privacy and the criminalisation of adult private cannabis use, possession
and cultivation.

The judgment did not create a general legal right to buy or sell cannabis.

Path must distinguish:

- private use
- private possession
- private cultivation
- purchase
- sale
- trafficking

These are not interchangeable concepts.

## 2024 Cannabis for Private Purposes Act

The Cannabis for Private Purposes Act 7 of 2024 was enacted following the
Constitutional Court's 2018 judgment.

The Act provides a framework concerning private adult cannabis use,
possession, cultivation and related matters.

It also provides for the expungement of criminal records for certain
qualifying cannabis-related offences.

## Criminal records

The historical significance of cannabis prohibition is not limited to
arrests.

A criminal process can produce consequences beyond the original offence,
including a criminal record.

For ClearPath, the relevant research chain is:

cannabis prohibition
→ policing
→ arrest
→ prosecution
→ conviction
→ criminal record
→ employment and social consequences
→ eligibility for record relief
→ expungement

Path must distinguish between:

- arrest
- charge
- prosecution
- conviction
- criminal record
- expungement

These terms describe different stages and must not be treated as synonyms.

## Evidence standard

For historical claims, Path should prefer:

1. primary legislation
2. court judgments
3. parliamentary records
4. official government records
5. archival police records
6. peer-reviewed historical research
7. reputable secondary sources

Claims about motives, racial impact, political purpose or policing practice
must be attributed to the relevant historical source where appropriate.

## Research safeguard

Do not state:

"Police used cannabis charges to lock up anyone who was making trouble"

as an established historical fact unless a source specifically supports that
claim.

A more defensible formulation is:

"Historical research indicates that cannabis policing operated within a wider
system of colonial and segregation-era policing and that cannabis possession
was sometimes discovered during police activity directed at other offences."

The underlying source and period should be provided.

## Research objective

The long-term objective is to construct a searchable historical evidence
base allowing Path to answer questions such as:

- When was cannabis criminalised?
- What laws criminalised cannabis?
- How many cannabis arrests were recorded?
- What proportion involved possession?
- How did policing strategies change?
- What did the 1952 committee recommend?
- How did apartheid-era policing interact with cannabis control?
- What did the Constitutional Court decide in 2018?
- What changed under the Cannabis for Private Purposes Act?
- Which historical convictions may be affected by later expungement mechanisms?
- What remains legally unresolved?
